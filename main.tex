\section{SNR of raw data}\label{sec:snr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.1 For an explanation of SNR, you can refer to the Canvas page for Learning Task
% on SNR estimation.

% Manual 2.3.1a Calculate the SNR for all four signals and tabulate them. Show using a figure
% how you calculated SNR. Comment on the important points to pay attention in calculating SNR.
\paragraph{(a)} The SNR is the ratio of signal power to noise power. It is calculated from the
spectrum of the whole recording ($N = 46648$). After removing the mean, the power per frequency
bin is $P[k] = 2|X[k]|^2/N^2$, so that $\sum_k P[k]$ is the mean power (Parseval). Power inside
the band $B_s$ = 0.5--40~Hz is signal; all other power (drift, 50~Hz, high-frequency noise) is noise
(Figure~\ref{fig:snr}). $B_s$ lies above most of the drift and below the 50~Hz mains, and contains
the heart rate of 1.4~Hz and its harmonics:
\[
  \mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{k \in B_s} P[k]}{\sum_{k \notin B_s} P[k]}.
\]
Unlike the Learning Task, the noise is not extrapolated from a signal-free band: that assumes
white noise, while the noise here is mainly drift below 0.5~Hz, which would then count as signal.
Table~\ref{tab:snr} gives the results. sig-F has the highest SNR of the three ECG signals and is
used in the rest of this report.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{snr_F}
  \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$ of sig-F (whole recording). Power in the shaded band counts as
  signal, the rest as noise.}
  \label{fig:snr}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{SNR of the raw and filtered signals.}
  \label{tab:snr}
  \begin{tabular}{lc}
    \toprule
    Signal & SNR (dB) \\
    \midrule
    sig-P & 13.6 \\
    sig-R & $-1.3$ \\
    sig-L & $-5.6$ \\
    sig-F & $-0.4$ \\
    \midrule
    sig-F, high-pass & 10.5 \\
    sig-F, high- + low-pass & 22.7 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Points to pay attention to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The SNR depends on the chosen signal band, so the same band must be used in every
        comparison.
  \item Noise inside the band counts as signal and ECG content outside it (e.g.\ QRS above
        40~Hz) counts as noise, so the SNR is only an estimate.
  \item The mean (DC offset) must be removed, otherwise it counts as power.
  \item The SNR depends on the length of the recording: for 10~s windows of sig-F it is
        3.5--9.7~dB, higher than for the whole recording, because a short window with its mean
        removed contains less slow drift.
\end{itemize}

\section{Design and application of high-pass filter}\label{sec:hp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.2a Design a high-pass filter to straighten the base line of the raw ECG signal
% with the highest SNR. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The baseline drift of sig-F lies below about 0.3~Hz and the heart rate is
$f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz (Phase 2), so the cut-off is placed between them at $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, the
lower edge of the signal band $B_s$. An IIR filter is used, because an FIR filter needs a
resolution $\Delta f = f_s/K$ below 0.5~Hz, i.e.\ $K > 2000$ taps at $f_s = 1024$~Hz, while an IIR
filter needs only a few coefficients. A Butterworth type is chosen for its flat passband (no ripple
on the ECG), and order 2 is enough to suppress the drift (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response}). It is
designed with the bilinear transform (\texttt{scipy.signal.butter}):
\[
  H(z) = 0.99783\,\frac{1 - 2z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 1.99566\,z^{-1} + 0.99567\,z^{-2}}.
\]
The IIR filter has a non-linear phase (one pass shifts the 1.4~Hz component by $30^\circ$), so it
is applied forward and backward: the total impulse response $h[n]*h[-n]$ is symmetric, so the
phase is zero. This is non-causal and not used in the reader (\S12.4), but it is allowed for
offline data and keeps the ECG shape and the correlation lags of
Sections~\ref{sec:acf}--\ref{sec:xcorr} unchanged. It squares the magnitude response, so the
effective response is $-6$~dB at $f_c$.

% Manual 2.3.2b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response} shows the effective magnitude response
$|H(f)|^2$: the drift region is attenuated by 19~dB (at 0.3~Hz) or more, while $f_\mathrm{HR}$ passes with
only $-0.1$~dB.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{hp_response}
  \caption{Effective magnitude response $|H(f)|^2$ of the high-pass filter (Butterworth, order 2,
  $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, applied forward and backward). Shaded: drift region below 0.3~Hz.}
  \label{fig:hp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2c Apply the filter on your selected ECG signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The filter is applied to the whole recording of sig-F in second-order-section
form, forward and backward (\texttt{scipy.signal.sosfiltfilt}).

% Manual 2.3.2d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered signal.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-time}) the baseline of the raw signal
wanders by more than 200~$\mu$V within 10~s, while the filtered baseline stays flat; the QRS
complexes keep their shape and amplitude. In the spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) the drift
power below 0.2~Hz drops by about 40~dB, while above about 1~Hz the raw and filtered spectra coincide, so the
heart-rate peaks, the 50~Hz line and the high-frequency noise are unchanged.

  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hp_time}
    \caption{Time domain, 35--45~s.}
    \label{fig:hp-time}
  \end{subfigure}
  \hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hp_freq}
    \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$, whole recording.}
    \label{fig:hp-freq}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{sig-F before and after high-pass filtering.}
  \label{fig:hp-signal}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2e Calculate the SNR of the filtered signal and append them to your previous SNR
% table. How does the SNR change after filtering? Comment on the influence of filter parameters
% to the changes in the SNR.
\paragraph{(e)} High-pass filtering raises the SNR of sig-F from $-0.4$~dB to 10.5~dB
(Table~\ref{tab:snr}), because the drift, which was most of the noise power, is removed while the
signal band is almost unchanged. For $f_c$ = 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7~Hz the SNR is 9.4, 10.5 and
10.6~dB at order 2 and 9.2, 10.7 and 10.7~dB at order 4. A cut-off below 0.5~Hz leaves drift
between $f_c$ and 0.5~Hz, which counts as noise; above 0.5~Hz the SNR hardly increases, because 95\%
of the remaining noise lies above 40~Hz (50~Hz mains and high-frequency noise), which a high-pass
filter cannot remove. Order 4 changes the SNR by at most 0.2~dB, since order 2 already
suppresses the drift.

\section{Design and application of low-pass filter}\label{sec:lp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.3a Design a low-pass filter to suppress the noise in the high-pass filtered signal.
% Carefully choose your parameters in such a way as not to modify the shape of the relevant
% signals too much. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The ECG harmonics of sig-F reach about 30~Hz (Phase~2), while the noise is the
50~Hz mains and broadband noise above it, so the cut-off is $f_c = 40$~Hz. To keep the shape of
the ECG, the filter is a linear-phase FIR, $y[n] = \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} h[i]\,x[n-i]$ with symmetric
$h[n]$: all frequencies are delayed by the same $(K-1)/2$ samples. It is designed with the window
method and a Hamming window, $w[n] = 0.54 - 0.46\cos\bigl(2\pi n/(K-1)\bigr)$, whose low side lobes
give about 53~dB stop-band attenuation. The window widens the transition band to about
$3.3\,\Delta f = 3.3 f_s/K$; $K = 201$ taps (odd, so the delay is a whole number of samples) gives
17~Hz, so the gain falls from 0~dB at 32~Hz to the
stop band at 48~Hz, just below the 50~Hz line. The delay of $(K-1)/2 = 100$ samples is removed by
applying the filter as a centred convolution, so it has zero phase.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{lp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude response of the low-pass FIR filter (Hamming window, $K = 201$,
  $f_c = 40$~Hz). The shaded band contains the ECG content.}
  \label{fig:lp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.3b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:lp-response} shows the magnitude response: 0~dB up to 32~Hz,
$-0.7$~dB at 35~Hz, $-6$~dB at $f_c = 40$~Hz and $-53.5$~dB at 50~Hz.

% Manual 2.3.3c Apply the filter on your high-pass filtered signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The high-pass filtered sig-F is convolved with $h[n]$ and the result is shifted
back by 100 samples, so it is aligned with the input.

% Manual 2.3.3d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered and high-pass filtered
% signals.
\paragraph{(d)} Figure~\ref{fig:lp-signal} compares the three signals. In the time domain the
fast noise on the base line is removed, while the P, QRS and T waves keep their shape and timing;
only the R peaks are about 12\,\% lower, because the QRS complex has some content above 40~Hz.
In the frequency domain the spectrum is unchanged up to 32~Hz and everything above 48~Hz, including
the 50~Hz line, drops by more than 50~dB; the drift below 0.5~Hz was already removed by the high-pass
filter.

% successive steps? Would a single band-pass filter give you advantage in performance?
\paragraph{(e)}

% Manual 2.3.3f Calculate the SNR of the low-pass filtered signal and append to your SNR table.

\paragraph{(f)} After both filters the SNR of sig-F is 22.7~dB (Table~\ref{tab:snr}), against
$-0.4$~dB raw and 10.5~dB after the high-pass filter alone. The high-pass filter removes the drift
below 0.5~Hz and the low-pass filter the 50~Hz mains and the noise above 40~Hz, so almost all power
outside the signal band is gone.

\section{Auto-correlation}\label{sec:acf}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.4a Calculate and plot the auto-correlations of the filtered signal. Comment on
% your observations in 2-3 sentences.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:acf-filt} shows the normalised auto-correlation
$R_{xx}[n]/R_{xx}[0]$ of the filtered (high- and low-pass) sig-F, calculated as in Phase 2 over
$t = 36.5$--41.5~s with the mean removed. It has narrow peaks at multiples of 0.72~s, the
heartbeat period (1.39~Hz), which decrease from 0.73 at 0.72~s to 0.28 at 3.58~s because fewer
samples overlap. Each peak is flanked by dips down to $-0.17$ at $\pm 0.075$~s, and between the
beats the curve stays close to zero.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{acf_filtered}
  \caption{Normalised auto-correlation of the filtered and unfiltered sig-F,
  $t = 36.5$--41.5~s. $R_{xx}$ is symmetric, so only positive lags are shown.}
  \label{fig:acf-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.4b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The shape and period are the same as for the unfiltered sig-F in Phase 2, as
the window was chosen with little drift. After filtering, the first side peak rises from 0.69
to 0.73, the later ones by 0.02--0.05, and the dips deepen from $-0.14$ to $-0.17$. The filters
remove 6\% of the power in the window (drift and high-frequency noise), which does not repeat
every beat: this lowers $R_{xx}[0]$ but not the side peaks, so the normalised side peaks rise
($0.69/0.94 = 0.73$).

% Manual 2.3.5a Calculate and plot the cross-correlation between sig-P and the filtered signal.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:xcorr-filt} shows the normalised cross-correlation
$R_{PF}[n]/\sqrt{R_{PP}[0]\,R_{FF}[0]}$ between sig-P and the high- and low-pass filtered sig-F,
over the same window as in Phase 2 ($t = 36.5$--41.5~s, mean removed). sig-P is left
unfiltered, since only the ECG is filtered, and sig-F is filtered over the whole recording
before the window is cut, so the filter start-up transients fall outside it.

% Manual 2.3.5b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The result is almost the same as in Phase 2: the maxima are 0.22 at $-0.25$~s
and 0.20 at $+0.42$~s (Phase 2: 0.22 and 0.19 at $-0.25$ and $+0.41$~s), and the period is
still 0.72~s. The lags do not change because both filters have zero phase, so the pulse still
follows the ECG spike by about 0.4~s. The values hardly rise because filtering removes only 6\% of the energy of sig-F in this window,
and the removed drift, 50~Hz and high-frequency noise did not match sig-P anyway.

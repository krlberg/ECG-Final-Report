\section{SNR of raw data}\label{sec:snr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.1 For an explanation of SNR, you can refer to the Canvas page for Learning Task
% on SNR estimation.

% Manual 2.3.1a Calculate the SNR for all four signals and tabulate them. Show using a figure
% how you calculated SNR. Comment on the important points to pay attention in calculating SNR.
\paragraph{(a)} The SNR of each signal is calculated from the spectrum of the whole recording
($N = 46648$ samples) in four steps (Figure~\ref{fig:snr}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The mean is removed, so the DC offset does not count as noise. The DFT $X[k]$ then
        splits the recording into frequency bins: bin $k$ lies at $f = k f_s/N$, so the bins are
        $f_s/N = 1024/46648 = 0.022$~Hz wide.
  \item The power in bin $k$ is $P[k] = 2|X[k]|^2/N^2$. Summed over all bins this gives the total
        power of the signal.
  \item Bins inside the band $B_s$ = 0.5--40~Hz count as signal, all other bins as noise. The noise is not extrapolated from a noise-only band, because it is not spread evenly over the
        frequencies but is mainly drift below 0.5~Hz.
  \item The SNR is the ratio of the two sums in dB between the signal power over the noise Power:
        \[
          \mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{k \in B_s} P[k]}{\sum_{k \notin B_s} P[k]}.
        \]
\end{enumerate}
The band edges are chosen from the spectra. Below 0.5~Hz the power is mainly baseline drift,
caused by breathing and electrode movement, while the heart rate lies at 1.40~Hz, so 0.5~Hz
separates the drift from the heartbeat. The harmonics of the heart rate, which together form \chg{the
shape of each heartbeat}, are visible up to about 30~Hz. Above them lies mostly noise, including the
50~Hz mains, so 40~Hz separates the ECG from the 50~Hz line. sig-P has almost no power above 7~Hz
(Figure~\ref{fig:snr}), so the same band can be used.

Table~\ref{tab:snr} gives the results. The highest SNR is that of sig-P, because the optical
pulse signal has much less drift below 0.5~Hz than the ECG leads (Figure~\ref{fig:snr}).
The filters are designed for the ECG signal with the highest SNR. sig-P is the pulse-oxi
signal and not an ECG lead; of the three ECG leads sig-F has the highest SNR, so it is used in the
rest of this report.

Besides removing the mean (step 1), two points need attention. The SNR depends on the
chosen band, so the same band is used for all signals, and it is only an estimate: noise inside
the band counts as signal, and ECG content outside it counts as noise. The SNR also depends on the length of the segment: for 10~s windows of sig-F it is
3.5--9.7~dB, because removing the mean of a short window also removes part of the slow drift.
The whole recording is therefore used.

\section{Design and application of high-pass filter}\label{sec:hp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.2a Design a high-pass filter to straighten the base line of the raw ECG signal
% with the highest SNR. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The high-pass filter is designed for sig-F, the ECG signal with the highest SNR
(Section~\ref{sec:snr}). The cut-off is $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, between the baseline drift below 0.5~Hz
and the heart rate $f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz. A linear-phase FIR filter is used, because it delays
all frequencies by the same $(K-1)/2$ samples, so the ECG keeps its shape and this delay is removed
afterwards. An IIR filter would need fewer coefficients, but its non-linear phase would change
the shape of the slow ECG waves. The filter is designed with the window method and a Hamming
window. The number of taps sets the steepness of the edge ($\Delta f = f_s/K$): with $K = 4097$
the drift at 0.3~Hz is attenuated by 17.6~dB while $f_\mathrm{HR}$ passes unchanged, whereas
$K = 2049$ gives only 10.5~dB.

% Manual 2.3.2b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response} shows the magnitude response: $-50$~dB at 0.1~Hz,
$-17.6$~dB at 0.3~Hz, $-6$~dB at $f_c$ and 0~dB at $f_\mathrm{HR}$.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{hp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude response of the high-pass FIR filter (Hamming window, $K = 4097$,
  $f_c = 0.5$~Hz).}
  \label{fig:hp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2c Apply the filter on your selected ECG signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The mean of sig-F is removed, the signal is convolved with $h[n]$ and the
result is shifted back by $(K-1)/2 = 2048$ samples. The results are shown in (d) and (e).

% Manual 2.3.2d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered signal.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-time}) the slow baseline drift of more
than 200~$\mu$V is removed, while \chg{the peaks of the heartbeats} keep their shape and amplitude. In the spectrum
(Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) the drift below 0.5~Hz drops by 14 to 40~dB, while everything above
1~Hz, including the heart-rate peaks and the 50~Hz line, is unchanged.

\paragraph{(e)} The SNR of sig-F rises from $-0.4$~dB to 10.6~dB (Table~\ref{tab:snr}),
because the drift was most of the noise power. A lower cut-off (0.3~Hz) or a shorter filter
($K = 2049$) gives about 9~dB, because more drift passes. A higher cut-off or a longer filter
adds at most 0.2~dB, because the remaining noise lies mainly above 40~Hz.

\section{Design and application of low-pass filter}\label{sec:lp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.3a Design a low-pass filter to suppress the noise in the high-pass filtered signal.
% Carefully choose your parameters in such a way as not to modify the shape of the relevant
% signals too much. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} \chg{The low-pass filter is designed for the high-pass filtered sig-F. The cut-off
is $f_c = 40$~Hz, between the ECG harmonics, which are clearly visible up to about 30~Hz, and the
noise above it: the 50~Hz mains and broadband noise. The same linear-phase FIR filter is used as for the high-pass
filter (Section~\ref{sec:hp}), because it delays all
frequencies by the same $(K-1)/2$ samples, so the ECG keeps its shape and this delay is removed
afterwards. The filter is designed with the window method and a Hamming window to reduce the
ripple. The number of taps sets the steepness of the edge ($\Delta f = f_s/K$): with $K = 201$
the 50~Hz line is attenuated by 53.5~dB while the ECG up to 32~Hz passes unchanged.}

% Manual 2.3.3b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:lp-response} shows the magnitude response with 0~dB up to 32~Hz,
$-0.7$~dB at 35~Hz, $-6$~dB at $f_c = 40$~Hz and $-53.5$~dB at 50~Hz.

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{lp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude response of the low-pass FIR filter (Hamming window, $K = 201$,
  $f_c = 40$~Hz).}
  \label{fig:lp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.3c Apply the filter on your high-pass filtered signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The high-pass filtered sig-F is convolved with $h[n]$ and the result is shifted
back by $(K-1)/2 = 100$ samples, so it lines up with the input. \chg{The results are shown in (d)
and (f).}

% Manual 2.3.3d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered and high-pass filtered
% signals.
\paragraph{(d)} \chg{Compared with the high-pass filtered signal,} the fast noise on the baseline is
gone \chg{in the time domain} (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-time}). \chg{The ECG keeps its shape and timing, but the highest peaks of the heartbeats are about
12\% lower, because these peaks are short and sharp and therefore contain frequencies above
40~Hz.} This is accepted, because a higher
cut-off would need a longer filter to still suppress the 50~Hz line. In the spectrum
(Figure~\ref{fig:lp-freq}) nothing changes up to 32~Hz, and from 50~Hz upwards, including the
50~Hz line, the power drops by about 40~dB. \chg{Compared with the unfiltered signal, the baseline drift is
also gone: the power below 0.5~Hz stays as low as after the high-pass filter.}

% Manual 2.3.3e Why are you allowed to apply the high-pass filter and the low-pass filter in two
% successive steps? Would a single band-pass filter give you advantage in performance?
\paragraph{(e)} \chg{The high-pass and the low-pass filter may be applied in two successive steps,
because both filters are linear and time-invariant, and convolution is commutative and
associative. The two steps therefore give the same output as one FIR band-pass filter with
$h[n] = h_\mathrm{HP}[n] \otimes h_\mathrm{LP}[n]$ ($4097 + 201 - 1 = 4297$ taps), or
$H(f) = H_\mathrm{HP}(f)\,H_\mathrm{LP}(f)$, and the order of the two steps does not matter.
A single band-pass filter does not give an advantage in performance, because it gives the same
output and takes about the same number of multiplications.}

% Manual 2.3.3f Calculate the SNR of the low-pass filtered signal and append to your SNR table.
% Compare your new SNR to the unfiltered and high-pass filtered SNR values.
\paragraph{(f)} \chg{After both filters the SNR of sig-F is 22.5~dB (Table~\ref{tab:snr}), against
$-0.4$~dB raw and 10.6~dB after the high-pass filter. The low-pass filter removes the 50~Hz mains
and the noise above 40~Hz. The remaining noise is mainly drift between 0.2 and 0.5~Hz, in the
edge of the high-pass filter. The value is an upper estimate, because noise inside 0.5--40~Hz
counts as signal.}

\section{Auto-correlation}\label{sec:acf}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.4a Calculate and plot the auto-correlations of the filtered signal. Comment on
% your observations in 2-3 sentences.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:acf-filt} shows the normalised auto-correlation
$R_{xx}[n]/R_{xx}[0]$ of the filtered sig-F (high- and low-pass), calculated over
$t = 36.5$--41.5~s with the mean removed. It has narrow peaks at multiples of the heartbeat
period of 0.72~s, because the \chg{short, sharp peaks of the heartbeats} only line up at whole periods, and the peaks
drop from 0.73 at 0.72~s to 0.28 at 3.58~s because fewer samples overlap. Each peak has dips
about 0.075~s on both sides (down to $-0.17$ next to the zero-lag peak), where \chg{the highest peak of each heartbeat lines
up with the negative dips just before and after it}; between the beats the curve stays
close to zero.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{acf_filtered}
  \caption{Normalised auto-correlation of the filtered and unfiltered sig-F,
  $t = 36.5$--41.5~s. $R_{xx}$ is symmetric, so only positive lags are shown.}
  \label{fig:acf-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.4b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The shape and period are the same as for the unfiltered sig-F in Phase 2,
because the window was chosen to have little drift. After filtering, the side peaks are
higher (the first rises from 0.69 to 0.73) and the dips deeper ($-0.14$ to $-0.17$). The
filters remove 6\% of the power in the window (drift and high-frequency noise). This noise is
different in every beat, so removing it lowers $R_{xx}[0]$ but not the side peaks, and the
normalised side peaks rise ($0.69/0.94 = 0.73$). The dips deepen further because the drift was
almost constant over 0.075~s, so it added a positive term at short lags.

\section{Cross-correlation}\label{sec:xcorr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.5a Calculate and plot the cross-correlation between sig-P and the filtered signal.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:xcorr-filt} shows the normalised cross-correlation
$R_{PF}[n]/\sqrt{R_{PP}[0]\,R_{FF}[0]}$ between the unfiltered sig-P and the filtered sig-F,
over $t = 36.5$--41.5~s with the mean removed. The filters are applied to the whole recording
before the window is cut, so their run-in \chg{at the edges} falls outside the window.

  delayed. Dots: the maxima and minimum quoted in (b).}
  \label{fig:xcorr-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.5b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} \chg{The result is close to that of Phase 2. The two largest maxima are 0.22 at
$-0.25$~s and 0.20 at $+0.42$~s (Phase 2: 0.22 at $-0.25$~s and 0.19 at $+0.41$~s). The maximum at
negative lag is the pulse of the previous beat, since a pulse cannot come before its own
heartbeat. The lags change by less than 0.01~s, because both filters have zero phase. The heights
of these maxima change by less than 0.01, because filtering removes only 6\% of the power of sig-F
in the window. Only the outer maxima and the minimum grow, by up to 0.03 (minimum $-0.25$ to
$-0.28$ at $-0.46$~s), because the slow drift, which shifted the curve up or down, is removed.}

% Manual 2.3.5c Compare your cross-correlation observations to your auto-correlation
% observations. What are your new observations beyond Phase 2?
\paragraph{(c)} Like the auto-correlation (Section~\ref{sec:acf}), the cross-correlation
repeats with the heartbeat, but its main peak is not at zero lag. A signal matches itself best
without a shift, while the pressure wave of each beat needs time to travel from the heart to the
finger, so the pulse peak follows \chg{the highest ECG peak} by about 0.4~s. The auto-correlation peaks are narrow, because the \chg{sharp ECG peaks} only line
up at whole periods. The cross-correlation peaks are broader and much lower (\chg{0.22} against 0.73),
because the smooth \chg{pulse signal (sig-P)} has a different shape from the ECG. Two observations are new
beyond Phase 2. \chg{First, filtering raises the auto-correlation side peak (0.69 to 0.73) but
leaves the main cross-correlation maxima unchanged, so their low value is caused by the different
shapes and not by noise. Second, the lags do not change, because both filters have zero phase, so
the delay of about 0.4~s between ECG and pulse comes from the signals and not from the
processing.}

% Manual 2.3.5d Conclude with a combined interpretation of the Fourier transform,
% auto-correlation and cross-correlation results.
\paragraph{(d)} The strong spectral peaks between 1 and 10~Hz (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) are
the heart rate $f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz and its harmonics, which together form the repeating \chg{shape of
each heartbeat}. The filters remove most of the drift below 0.5~Hz, the 50~Hz line and the noise
above 40~Hz (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-signal}), which raises the SNR of sig-F from $-0.4$ to \chg{22.5}~dB
(Table~\ref{tab:snr}). The auto-correlation shows the same rhythm in the time domain, with a
period of 0.72~s, which matches $f_\mathrm{HR}$. \chg{The cross-correlation has the same period and
shows that the pulse peak follows the highest ECG peak by about 0.4~s. The filters do not change this
delay, because both have zero phase.}

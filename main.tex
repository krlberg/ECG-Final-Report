\section{SNR of raw data}\label{sec:snr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.1 For an explanation of SNR, you can refer to the Canvas page for Learning Task
% on SNR estimation.

% Manual 2.3.1a Calculate the SNR for all four signals and tabulate them. Show using a figure
% how you calculated SNR. Comment on the important points to pay attention in calculating SNR.
\paragraph{(a)} \chg{The SNR of each signal is calculated from the spectrum of the whole recording
($N = 46648$ samples) in four steps (Figure~\ref{fig:snr}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The mean is removed, so the DC offset does not count as noise. The DFT $X[k]$ then
        splits the recording into frequency bins: bin $k$ lies at $f = k f_s/N$, so the bins are
        $f_s/N = 0.022$~Hz wide.
  \item The power in bin $k$ is $P[k] = 2|X[k]|^2/N^2$. Summed over all bins this gives the total
        power of the signal.
  \item Bins inside the band $B_s$ = 0.5--40~Hz count as signal, all other bins as noise.
  \item The SNR is the ratio of the two sums in dB:
        \[
          \mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{k \in B_s} P[k]}{\sum_{k \notin B_s} P[k]}.
        \]
\end{enumerate}
The band edges are chosen from the spectra. Below 0.5~Hz the power is mainly baseline drift,
caused by breathing and electrode movement, while the heart rate lies at 1.40~Hz, so 0.5~Hz
separates the drift from the heartbeat. The harmonics of the heart rate, which together form the
P, QRS and T waves, are visible up to about 30~Hz. Above them lies mostly noise, including the
50~Hz mains, so 40~Hz separates the ECG from the 50~Hz line. For sig-P only 0.2\% of the power lies in 7--40~Hz,
so the same band can be used.}

Table~\ref{tab:snr} gives the results. The highest SNR is that of sig-P, because the optical
pulse signal has little drift (4\% of its power lies below 0.5~Hz, against 49\% for sig-F). Of the
three ECG signals sig-F has the highest SNR, so it is used in the rest of this report.

\chg{Important points when calculating the SNR:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Band.} The SNR depends on the chosen band, so all signals use the same one. It is
        an estimate: noise inside the band counts as signal, and ECG content outside it (e.g.\
        part of the QRS above 40~Hz) counts as noise.
  \item \textbf{DC offset.} The mean (about 4700~$\mu$V in sig-F) is removed first, otherwise it
        counts as noise.
  \item \textbf{Segment length.} For 10~s windows of sig-F the SNR is 3.5--9.7~dB, because
        removing the mean of a short window also removes part of the slow drift. The whole
        recording is therefore used.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Design and application of high-pass filter}\label{sec:hp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.2a Design a high-pass filter to straighten the base line of the raw ECG signal
% with the highest SNR. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The baseline drift of sig-F, caused by breathing and electrode movement, lies
mainly below 0.5~Hz and the heart rate is $f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz, so the cut-off is placed
at $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, the lower edge of $B_s$. An FIR filter would need a resolution
$\Delta f = f_s/K$ below 0.5~Hz, so $K > 2000$ taps. An IIR filter needs only a few
coefficients and is used instead: a Butterworth filter designed with the bilinear transform
(\texttt{scipy.signal.butter}), because its pass band is flat. This gives
\[
  H(z) = 0.99783\,\frac{1 - 2z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 1.99566\,z^{-1} + 0.99567\,z^{-2}}.
\]
The phase of an IIR filter is non-linear, but the shift becomes small well above $f_c$:
$f_\mathrm{HR}$ is shifted by $30^\circ$ (60~ms) and 10~Hz by only $4^\circ$, so the QRS complex
keeps its shape. A higher order gives a steeper edge but a larger phase shift, so order 2 is
used.

% Manual 2.3.2b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response} shows the magnitude and phase response. The drift is
attenuated by 9.4~dB at 0.3~Hz and more at lower frequencies, the response at $f_c$ is $-3$~dB, and
$f_\mathrm{HR}$ passes with $-0.1$~dB.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{hp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude and phase response of the high-pass filter (Butterworth, order 2,
  $f_c = 0.5$~Hz).}
  \label{fig:hp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2c Apply the filter on your selected ECG signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The filter is applied once, as a causal difference equation
(\texttt{scipy.signal.lfilter}), to the whole recording of sig-F. The mean is removed first,
otherwise the DC offset gives a large transient at the start of the output.

% Manual 2.3.2d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered signal.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-time}) the raw baseline wanders by more
than 200~$\mu$V within 10~s, while the filtered baseline stays around zero and the QRS complexes
keep their shape and amplitude. In the spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) the drift power
drops by about 30~dB below 0.2~Hz and by 8~dB between 0.2 and 0.5~Hz. Above 1~Hz, including
the heart-rate peaks and the 50~Hz line, the spectrum hardly changes.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hp_time}
    \caption{Time domain, 35--45~s (raw: mean removed).}
    \label{fig:hp-time}
  \end{subfigure}
  \hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hp_freq}
    \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$, whole recording.}
    \label{fig:hp-freq}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Raw and high-pass filtered sig-F.}
  \label{fig:hp-signal}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2e Calculate the SNR of the filtered signal and append them to your previous SNR
% table. How does the SNR change after filtering? Comment on the influence of filter parameters
% to the changes in the SNR.
\paragraph{(e)} High-pass filtering raises the SNR of sig-F from $-0.4$~dB to 10.0~dB
(Table~\ref{tab:snr}), because the drift was most of the noise power. A lower cut-off
(0.3~Hz) gives 8.5~dB, because more drift stays in the noise. A higher cut-off (0.7~Hz) or
order 4 adds only 0.5~dB, because 82\% of the remaining noise lies above 40~Hz (50~Hz mains
and high-frequency noise), where the high-pass filter has no effect.

\section{Design and application of low-pass filter}\label{sec:lp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.3a Design a low-pass filter to suppress the noise in the high-pass filtered signal.
% Carefully choose your parameters in such a way as not to modify the shape of the relevant
% signals too much. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The ECG harmonics of sig-F are clearly visible up to about 30~Hz and the noise
is the 50~Hz mains and broadband noise above it (e.g.\ muscle activity), so the cut-off is set
to $f_c = 40$~Hz. To keep the shape of the ECG, a linear-phase FIR filter is used, which
delays all frequencies by the same $(K-1)/2$ samples. It is designed with the
window method (\texttt{scipy.signal.firwin}) and a Hamming window to reduce the ripple. The
number of taps sets the steepness ($\Delta f = f_s/K$); $K = 201$ gives $-53.5$~dB at the
50~Hz line.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{lp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude response of the low-pass FIR filter (Hamming window, $K = 201$,
  $f_c = 40$~Hz).}
  \label{fig:lp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.3b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:lp-response} shows the magnitude response with 0~dB up to 32~Hz,
$-0.7$~dB at 35~Hz, $-6$~dB at $f_c = 40$~Hz and $-53.5$~dB at 50~Hz.

% Manual 2.3.3c Apply the filter on your high-pass filtered signal.
\paragraph{(c)} The high-pass filtered sig-F is convolved with $h[n]$ and the result is shifted
back by $(K-1)/2 = 100$ samples, so it lines up with the input.

% Manual 2.3.3d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered and high-pass filtered
% signals.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-time}) the fast noise on the baseline is
gone. The P, QRS and T waves keep their shape and timing, but the R peaks are about 13\% lower,
because the QRS complex has some content above 40~Hz. This is accepted, because a higher
cut-off would need a longer filter to still suppress the 50~Hz line. In the spectrum
(Figure~\ref{fig:lp-freq}) nothing changes up to 32~Hz, and from 50~Hz upwards, including the
50~Hz line, the power drops by about 40~dB. The drift below 0.5~Hz is the same as after the
high-pass filter.

  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lp_time}
    \caption{Time domain, 38--40.5~s (raw: mean removed).}
    \label{fig:lp-time}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lp_freq}
    \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$, whole recording.}
    \label{fig:lp-freq}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Raw, high-pass filtered (HP) and high- and low-pass filtered (HP + LP) sig-F.}
  \label{fig:lp-signal}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.3e Why are you allowed to apply the high-pass filter and the low-pass filter in two
% successive steps? Would a single band-pass filter give you advantage in performance?
\paragraph{(e)} Both filters are linear and time-invariant, and convolution is commutative and
associative. The two steps are therefore equal to one band-pass filter with
$h[n] = h_\mathrm{HP}[n] \otimes h_\mathrm{LP}[n]$, or $H(f) = H_\mathrm{HP}(f)\,H_\mathrm{LP}(f)$,
in either order. A single band-pass filter gives the same output, so it has no advantage in
performance; as an FIR filter it would need $K > 2000$ taps (Section~\ref{sec:hp}).

% Manual 2.3.3f Calculate the SNR of the low-pass filtered signal and append to your SNR table.
% Compare your new SNR to the unfiltered and high-pass filtered SNR values.
\paragraph{(f)} After both filters the SNR of sig-F is 17.2~dB (Table~\ref{tab:snr}), against
$-0.4$~dB raw and 10.0~dB after the high-pass filter. The low-pass filter removes the 50~Hz
mains and the noise above 40~Hz. The remaining noise is mainly drift below 0.5~Hz that the
high-pass filter did not fully remove. The value is an upper estimate, because noise inside
0.5--40~Hz counts as signal.

\section{Auto-correlation}\label{sec:acf}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.4a Calculate and plot the auto-correlations of the filtered signal. Comment on
% your observations in 2-3 sentences.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:acf-filt} shows the normalised auto-correlation
$R_{xx}[n]/R_{xx}[0]$ of the filtered sig-F (high- and low-pass), calculated over
$t = 36.5$--41.5~s with the mean removed. It has narrow peaks at multiples of the heartbeat
period of 0.72~s, because the short QRS complexes only line up at whole periods, and the peaks
drop from 0.73 at 0.72~s to 0.28 at 3.58~s because fewer samples overlap. Each peak has dips
about 0.075~s on both sides (down to $-0.17$ next to the zero-lag peak), where the R peaks line
up with the negative parts on both sides of the QRS complex; between the beats the curve stays
close to zero.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{acf_filtered}
  \caption{Normalised auto-correlation of the filtered and unfiltered sig-F,
  $t = 36.5$--41.5~s. $R_{xx}$ is symmetric, so only positive lags are shown.}
  \label{fig:acf-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.4b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The shape and period are the same as for the unfiltered sig-F in Phase 2,
because the window was chosen to have little drift. After filtering, the side peaks are
higher (the first rises from 0.69 to 0.73) and the dips deeper ($-0.14$ to $-0.17$). The
filters remove 6\% of the power in the window (drift and high-frequency noise). This noise is
different in every beat, so removing it lowers $R_{xx}[0]$ but not the side peaks, and the
normalised side peaks rise ($0.69/0.94 = 0.73$). The dips deepen further because the drift was
almost constant over 0.075~s, so it added a positive term at short lags.

\section{Cross-correlation}\label{sec:xcorr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.5a Calculate and plot the cross-correlation between sig-P and the filtered signal.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:xcorr-filt} shows the normalised cross-correlation
$R_{PF}[n]/\sqrt{R_{PP}[0]\,R_{FF}[0]}$ between the unfiltered sig-P and the filtered sig-F,
over $t = 36.5$--41.5~s with the mean removed. The filters are applied to the whole recording
before the window is cut, so their run-in at the start falls outside the window.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{xcorr_filtered}
  \caption{Normalised cross-correlation between sig-P and the filtered sig-F (solid), with the
  Phase 2 result for the raw sig-F (dashed), $t = 36.5$--41.5~s. A positive lag means sig-P is
  delayed. Dots: the maxima and minimum quoted in (b).}
  \label{fig:xcorr-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.5b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The period of 0.72~s is the same as in Phase 2, but the maxima are lower and
shifted. The two largest are 0.19 at $-0.21$~s and 0.16 at $+0.51$~s (Phase 2: 0.22 at
$-0.25$~s and 0.19 at $+0.41$~s). The maximum at negative lag is the pulse of the previous
beat, since a pulse cannot come before its own heartbeat. The deepest minimum goes from $-0.25$
to $-0.34$ (at $-0.44$~s).

Both maxima move by 0.04--0.10~s to larger lags. The high-pass filter shifts the slow parts of
the ECG earlier in time (60~ms at $f_\mathrm{HR}$ and more at lower frequencies), while sig-P is
not filtered, so sig-P appears more delayed. Because this shift is different for each
frequency, it also changes the shape of the slow ECG wave, which dominates the correlation
with the smooth pulse wave, so the maxima drop and the minimum deepens.

% Manual 2.3.5c Compare your cross-correlation observations to your auto-correlation
% observations. What are your new observations beyond Phase 2?
\paragraph{(c)} Like the auto-correlation (Section~\ref{sec:acf}), the cross-correlation
repeats with the heartbeat, but its main peak is not at zero lag. A signal matches itself best
without a shift, while the pressure wave of each beat needs time to travel from the heart to the
finger, so the pulse peak follows the R peak by about 0.4~s. The auto-correlation peaks are narrow, because the sharp QRS spikes only line
up at whole periods. The cross-correlation peaks are broader and much lower (0.19 against 0.73),
because the smooth pulse wave has a different shape from the ECG. Two observations are new
beyond Phase 2. First, filtering raises the auto-correlation side peak (0.69 to 0.73) but not
the cross-correlation maxima (they even drop, see (b)), so their low value is caused by the
different shapes and not by noise.
Second, the phase shift of the high-pass filter does not affect the auto-correlation, which
compares sig-F with itself, but it shifts the cross-correlation with the unfiltered sig-P.

% Manual 2.3.5d Conclude with a combined interpretation of the Fourier transform,
% auto-correlation and cross-correlation results.
\paragraph{(d)} The strong spectral peaks between 1 and 10~Hz (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) are
the heart rate $f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz and its harmonics, which together form the repeating P,
QRS and T waves. The filters remove most of the drift below 0.5~Hz, the 50~Hz line and the noise
above 40~Hz (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-signal}), which raises the SNR of sig-F from $-0.4$ to 17.2~dB
(Table~\ref{tab:snr}). The auto-correlation shows the same rhythm in the time domain, with a
period of 0.72~s, which matches $f_\mathrm{HR}$. The cross-correlation has the same period. For
the unfiltered sig-F its maximum at $+0.41$~s shows that the pulse peak follows the ECG spike by
about 0.4~s. With the filtered sig-F it appears at
0.51~s because of the phase shift of the high-pass filter, so the delay is best read from the
unfiltered signals.

\section{SNR of raw data}\label{sec:snr}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.1 For an explanation of SNR, you can refer to the Canvas page for Learning Task
% on SNR estimation.

% Manual 2.3.1a Calculate the SNR for all four signals and tabulate them. Show using a figure
% how you calculated SNR. Comment on the important points to pay attention in calculating SNR.
\paragraph{(a)} We calculate the SNR from the spectrum of the whole recording ($N = 46648$).
After removing the mean, the power per frequency bin is $P[k] = 2|X[k]|^2/N^2$. Power in the band
$B_s$ = 0.5--40~Hz counts as signal and all other power as noise (Figure~\ref{fig:snr}). This band
contains the heart rate (1.40~Hz) and its harmonics, and leaves out most of the drift and the
50~Hz mains:
\[
  \mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{k \in B_s} P[k]}{\sum_{k \notin B_s} P[k]}.
\]
Table~\ref{tab:snr} gives the results. Of the three ECG signals sig-F has the highest SNR, so we use it in the rest of this report.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{snr_F}
  \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$ of sig-F (whole recording). Bins inside $B_s$ count as signal,
  the rest as noise.}
  \label{fig:snr}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{SNR of the raw and filtered signals.}
  \label{tab:snr}
  \begin{tabular}{lc}
    \toprule
    Signal & SNR (dB) \\
    \midrule
    sig-P & 13.6 \\
    sig-R & $-1.3$ \\
    sig-L & $-5.6$ \\
    sig-F & $-0.4$ \\
    \midrule
    sig-F, high-pass & 10.5 \\
    sig-F, high- + low-pass & 22.7 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Points to pay attention to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The SNR depends on the chosen band, so every comparison must use the same band. Noise
        inside the band counts as signal and ECG content outside it (e.g.\ QRS above 40~Hz)
        counts as noise, so the SNR is only an estimate.
  \item The mean (DC offset, about 4700~$\mu$V in sig-F) must be removed, otherwise it counts as noise and is far larger than the drift.
  \item We do not estimate the noise from a signal-free band as in the Learning Task. That
        assumes white noise, but here the noise is mainly drift below 0.5~Hz.
  \item The SNR depends on the length of the recording. For 10~s windows of sig-F it is
        3.5--9.7~dB, because a short window with its mean removed contains less slow drift.
\end{itemize}

\section{Design and application of high-pass filter}\label{sec:hp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.2a Design a high-pass filter to straighten the base line of the raw ECG signal
% with the highest SNR. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The baseline drift of sig-F lies below about 0.3~Hz and the heart rate is
$f_\mathrm{HR} = 1.40$~Hz (Phase 2), so we put the cut-off between them at $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, the
lower edge of $B_s$. We use an IIR filter, because an FIR filter would need
$\Delta f = f_s/K < 0.5$~Hz, so $K > 2000$ taps at $f_s = 1024$~Hz, while an IIR filter needs
only a few coefficients. We chose a Butterworth filter because its flat passband adds no ripple
to the ECG, and order 2 because it is enough to suppress the drift
(Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response}). The bilinear transform (\texttt{scipy.signal.butter}) gives
\[
  H(z) = 0.99783\,\frac{1 - 2z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 1.99566\,z^{-1} + 0.99567\,z^{-2}}.
\]
The phase of this filter is non-linear (one pass shifts $f_\mathrm{HR}$ by $30^\circ$), so
we apply it forward and backward. The total impulse response $h[n]\otimes h[-n]$ is then symmetric and
the phase is zero. This is non-causal, which is fine for recorded data, and it keeps the ECG
shape and the correlation lags of Sections~\ref{sec:acf}--\ref{sec:xcorr} unchanged. The two
passes square the magnitude response, so the effective response at $f_c$ is $-6$~dB.

% Manual 2.3.2b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:hp-response} shows the effective magnitude response
$|H(f)|^2$. The drift region is attenuated by at least 19~dB (at 0.3~Hz), and $f_\mathrm{HR}$
passes with $-0.1$~dB.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{hp_response}
  \caption{Effective magnitude response $|H(f)|^2$ of the high-pass filter (Butterworth, order 2,
  $f_c = 0.5$~Hz, applied forward and backward).}
  \label{fig:hp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2c Apply the filter on your selected ECG signal.
\paragraph{(c)} We apply the filter forward and backward to the whole recording of sig-F, in
second-order-section form (\texttt{scipy.signal.sosfiltfilt}).

% Manual 2.3.2d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered signal.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-time}) the raw baseline wanders by more
than 200~$\mu$V within 10~s, while the filtered baseline is flat and the QRS complexes keep
their shape and amplitude. In the spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:hp-freq}) the drift power below
0.2~Hz drops by about 40~dB. Above about 1~Hz, including the heart-rate peaks and the 50~Hz
line, the spectrum does not change.

    \label{fig:hp-time}

  \hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \centering

    \caption{Power per bin $P[k]$, whole recording.}
    \label{fig:hp-freq}
  \end{subfigure}

  \label{fig:hp-signal}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.2e Calculate the SNR of the filtered signal and append them to your previous SNR
% table. How does the SNR change after filtering? Comment on the influence of filter parameters
% to the changes in the SNR.
\paragraph{(e)} High-pass filtering raises the SNR of sig-F from $-0.4$~dB to 10.5~dB
(Table~\ref{tab:snr}), because the drift was most of the noise power. For $f_c$ = 0.3, 0.5 and
0.7~Hz the SNR is 9.4, 10.5 and 10.6~dB at order 2, and 9.2, 10.7 and 10.7~dB at order 4. A
cut-off below 0.5~Hz leaves the drift between $f_c$ and 0.5~Hz in the noise. A higher cut-off
hardly helps, because 95\% of the remaining noise lies above 40~Hz (50~Hz mains and
high-frequency noise). Order 4 changes the SNR by at most 0.2~dB, since order 2 already
suppresses the drift.

\section{Design and application of low-pass filter}\label{sec:lp}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.3a Design a low-pass filter to suppress the noise in the high-pass filtered signal.
% Carefully choose your parameters in such a way as not to modify the shape of the relevant
% signals too much. Clearly motivate the choices you made in the design of the filter.
\paragraph{(a)} The ECG harmonics of sig-F reach about 30~Hz (Phase~2) and the noise is the
50~Hz mains and broadband noise above it, so we set $f_c = 40$~Hz. To keep the shape of the ECG
we use a linear-phase FIR filter, $y[n] = \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} h[i]\,x[n-i]$ with symmetric $h[n]$,
which delays all frequencies by the same $(K-1)/2$ samples. We use the window method with a
Hamming window, because its low side lobes give about 53~dB stop-band attenuation. The window
widens the transition band to about $3.3 f_s/K$, which is 17~Hz for $K = 201$ taps. The gain
then falls from 0~dB at 32~Hz to the stop band at 48~Hz, just below the 50~Hz line. $K$ is odd,
so the delay of 100 samples is a whole number of samples, and we remove it with a centred
convolution. The filter then has zero phase.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{lp_response}
  \caption{Magnitude response of the low-pass FIR filter (Hamming window, $K = 201$,
  $f_c = 40$~Hz).}
  \label{fig:lp-response}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.3b Present the filter characteristics in an annotated graph in the frequency
% domain.
\paragraph{(b)} Figure~\ref{fig:lp-response} shows the magnitude response: 0~dB up to 32~Hz,
$-0.7$~dB at 35~Hz, $-6$~dB at $f_c = 40$~Hz and $-53.5$~dB at 50~Hz.

% Manual 2.3.3c Apply the filter on your high-pass filtered signal.
\paragraph{(c)} We convolve the high-pass filtered sig-F with $h[n]$ and shift the result back
by 100 samples, so it lines up with the input.

% Manual 2.3.3d Present the filtered signal in the time-domain as well as in the frequency
% domain. Comment on the changes you observe with respect to unfiltered and high-pass filtered
% signals.
\paragraph{(d)} In the time domain (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-time}) the fast noise on the baseline is
gone. The P, QRS and T waves keep their shape and timing, but the R peaks are about 12\% lower,
because the QRS complex has some content above 40~Hz. In the spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:lp-freq})
nothing changes up to 32~Hz, and everything above 48~Hz, including the 50~Hz line, drops by more
than 50~dB. The drift below 0.5~Hz was already removed by the high-pass filter.

% successive steps? Would a single band-pass filter give you advantage in performance?
\paragraph{(e)}

% Manual 2.3.3f Calculate the SNR of the low-pass filtered signal and append to your SNR table.

\paragraph{(f)} After both filters the SNR of sig-F is 22.7~dB (Table~\ref{tab:snr}), against
$-0.4$~dB raw and 10.5~dB after the high-pass filter. The low-pass filter removes the 50~Hz mains
and the noise above 40~Hz, which the high-pass filter left in, so almost all power outside the
signal band is gone.

\section{Auto-correlation}\label{sec:acf}
% =====================================================================
% Manual 2.3.4a Calculate and plot the auto-correlations of the filtered signal. Comment on
% your observations in 2-3 sentences.
\paragraph{(a)} Figure~\ref{fig:acf-filt} shows the normalised auto-correlation
$R_{xx}[n]/R_{xx}[0]$ of the filtered sig-F, calculated as in Phase 2 over $t = 36.5$--41.5~s
with the mean removed. It has narrow peaks at multiples of the heartbeat period of 0.72~s, which
drop from 0.73 at 0.72~s to 0.28 at 3.58~s because fewer samples overlap. Next to each peak are
dips down to $-0.17$ at $\pm 0.075$~s, and between the beats the curve stays close to zero.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{acf_filtered}
  \caption{Normalised auto-correlation of the filtered and unfiltered sig-F,
  $t = 36.5$--41.5~s. $R_{xx}$ is symmetric, so only positive lags are shown.}
  \label{fig:acf-filt}
\end{figure}

% Manual 2.3.4b Compare your new correlation result with the unfiltered case in Phase 2.
\paragraph{(b)} The shape and period are the same as for the unfiltered sig-F in Phase 2,
because the window was chosen to have little drift. After filtering, the side peaks are slightly
higher (the first rises from 0.69 to 0.73) and the dips deepen from $-0.14$ to $-0.17$. The
filters remove 6\% of the power in the window (drift and high-frequency noise). This power does
not repeat every beat, so removing it lowers $R_{xx}[0]$ but not the side peaks, which raises the
normalised peaks ($0.69/0.94 = 0.73$).
